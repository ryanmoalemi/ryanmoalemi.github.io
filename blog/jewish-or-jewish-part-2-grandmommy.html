Jewish or Jew-ish? · Part 2 of 3

What Grandmommy Remembered

November 6, 2026 · Ryan Moalemi

At the end of Part 1, I was trying to work out whether my great-grandmother Dorothy Armstrong was Jewish. Ancestry had given me names and conflicting records, but I wanted to hear what somebody in my family actually remembered.

So I went to visit Grandmommy.

Her name is Pamela, and Dorothy was her mother. When I asked about our Jewish heritage, she told me she had always known her mother was Jewish. She also told me that both of Dorothy's parents were Jewish.

That was a much more direct answer than anything I had found in the family tree. But there was something else: Dorothy did not like to talk about it.

Why she kept it quiet

Grandmommy got emotional telling me why she thought her mother avoided the subject. She believed Dorothy was afraid that her daughters, Pamela and Jane, would be discriminated against. Keeping their Jewish identity quiet, she thought, was a way to keep them safe.

That was the part that stayed with me. I had grown up hearing that we had Jewish heritage, but I hadn't understood why I knew so little about it.

I had been focused on whether the family story was true. Grandmommy had always known it was true. What she remembered was her mother not wanting to discuss it.

I can't ask Dorothy to explain that decision herself. What I have is her daughter's account, and my grandmother understood it as her mother trying to protect her girls.

It made me think differently about the missing information. I had been treating it as something I needed to track down. Now I had to consider that some of it might have been left unsaid on purpose.

What she remembered about her grandmother

I also asked my grandmother about her own maternal grandmother, Dorothy's mother. In my Ancestry research, Dorothy's mother appears as Jeannette E Tuck.

The only description I could get was that my grandmother remembered a grandmother as “mean.”

Well, that was direct.

I was hoping for more detail, but that was about as much as I got. After spending so much time looking at Jeannette's name on a screen, it was interesting to hear that from my grandmother, even if the review wasn't great.

My grandmother had more to say about her mother. She knew Dorothy was Jewish, knew Dorothy's parents were Jewish, and remembered that Dorothy did not want to talk about it. Those were the details I wanted to get down correctly.

Shapiro became Spears

Another family detail helped connect the names I had been finding: Dorothy's father had changed his surname from Shapiro to Spears.

My tree identifies him as Benjamin Herman Shapiro. The family account is that he later used Spears, which explains why both names kept coming up in the research.

Census records on Ancestry appear to line up with my grandmother's memory: the family is listed as Shapiro in the 1920 census (as “Benj H Shapiro”) and in the 1925 census, and as Spears in the 1930 and 1940 censuses. Find a Grave lists a Benjamin Herman Spears (February 22, 1892 to November 21, 1951), though I have not confirmed that this is the same person.

I don't yet have a name-change petition or a document explaining his reason. But after hearing my grandmother's account, I wanted to understand more about why Jewish families changed their names during that period.

Historian Kirsten Fermaglich researched New York name-change petitions for her book A Rosenberg by Any Other Name. She found that thousands of Jewish people legally changed their names in response to antisemitism. Many remained active in Jewish life. Changing the name did not necessarily mean giving up the identity. source

That gave me some context for Shapiro becoming Spears. It does not establish why Dorothy's father made the change, but it makes the question worth pursuing.

The discrimination was real

One example I found was right here in the San Diego area.

La Jolla had housing practices that excluded Jewish buyers. The La Jolla Historical Society dates the breakdown of that arrangement to 1957–1959, when plans for a University of California campus ran into the problem of Jewish faculty being unable to buy homes there. source

Learning that made my grandmother's explanation easier to understand. Fear of discrimination was not something I had to imagine. There were documented reasons a Jewish parent might worry about how her children would be treated.

I don't know which experiences shaped Dorothy's thinking, and I am not suggesting she personally encountered those particular housing restrictions. But the history gave me a better understanding of the concern my grandmother was describing.

What the visit changed

I started this search partly because I had been inspired by younger Jews in the business community openly embracing their heritage. They were wrapping tefillin, getting together, and proudly taking part in their traditions.

Then Grandmommy told me about her mother, who, she believed, had kept the same identity quiet to protect her daughters.

I hadn't expected that contrast to become part of the story. I was looking for a connection I could do more with. She remembered a mother who had apparently worried about what it might mean for her children to make that connection visible.

There are still records to sort out, including the conflicting Dorothy profiles. But I now have more than names on a screen. I have my grandmother's account of her mother and grandparents, and her explanation for why this part of our history was so rarely discussed.

I'm glad I asked her.
